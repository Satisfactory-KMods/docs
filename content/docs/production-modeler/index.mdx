The Production Modeler is a drawing board for factories. You can place the
buildings yourself or generate an editable first chain, connect them the way the
belts run, and let the modeler work out the rates.

:::caution[Alpha]
The modeler is still in alpha. Models you save are kept, but the catalog behind
them changes as the game data does, and features are still landing.
:::

## Drawing or planning

The modeler starts from what you have in mind. You already know roughly what you
want to build, and you want to see whether it adds up. Every node is yours, and
the solver only fills in the numbers that follow from what you placed.

When you would rather start from a target, choose **Initial automatic plan**
while creating the model or use **Plan automatically (Exp)** from the palette.
That path is described under [Plan a chain automatically](#plan-a-chain-automatically)
and it is experimental: it picks a recipe per step, and the result is a starting
point you edit like anything else you drew.

## Plan a chain automatically

Choose **Initial automatic plan** on the creation page or open the palette and
choose **Plan automatically (Exp)**. The wizard has two starts: pick an output
item and let the modeler choose its first recipe, or pin a starting recipe and
choose one of its outputs. Set the amount per minute and the modeler builds the
chain behind it: one node per step, connected, with a miner or extractor on
every raw input.

The optional first step accepts a Satisfactory `.sav` file. Its unlocked recipe
paths are matched exactly against the loaded catalog, and the resulting recipe
and production-machine filters stay with the model. They constrain the wizard,
later automatic plans, catalog-backed palette entries and machine choices. The
palette shows when this filter is active and lets you remove it.

Only the matched catalog IDs are saved. The save binary is processed in memory
and is not stored in the model or browser draft. In the web app it goes to the
public savegame analyzer; the desktop app parses it locally and can do so
offline.

Only the first node carries the rate you asked for. Everything behind it is
sized by the solver, so a clock, a limit or a machine you change afterwards
still wins, exactly as if you had drawn the chain yourself.

A raw resource is mined, not crafted: when a machine can pull the part out of
the map, the chain ends at that extractor instead of walking into an ore
conversion recipe. Packaging and unpackaging pairs are avoided the same way, so
asking for fuel gives you a refinery and not a canister round trip.

Two switches change what it builds. Alternate recipes are off by default, so the
chain uses the recipes a fresh save has; turn them on and an alternate is picked
whenever it is the simpler step. Attaching resource nodes is on by default; turn
it off to leave every raw input open and wire your own extraction in.

The plan says what it could not finish. Inputs it left open are listed in the
messages panel, along with anything that fed its own chain and the note that a
very deep chain was cut short at its node budget.

Planning happens on your own machine, from the catalog the modeler already
loaded, so it also works in the desktop app with no connection at all. The whole
chain arrives as a single step, so one undo takes all of it back.

A wiki page can hand a part straight over: the plan button on a recipe or an item
opens the modeler with that part already filled in.

## The language you read it in

Part, machine and recipe names follow the language you picked for the site. The
modeler asks the service for the catalog in that language and swaps it out when
you switch, so nothing has to be reloaded by hand. The desktop app and an
imported game-data pack follow the same language, as long as the pack carries
its translations. Text the game data was never translated into stays English,
and so does a pack exported before translations existed.

## Your first model

Open the modeler and choose **Empty model** for an empty canvas, **Initial
automatic plan** for the wizard, or one of the import actions. Importing a
savegame creates a model with its unlock filter and any exactly recognized
resource extractors; it does not guess production recipes from built machines.

Double click empty space to open the palette, or right click for the same menu.
Search by recipe name, by input, or by output, and toggle those three fields
individually. Typing several words matches all of them; if that finds nothing,
the palette falls back to matching any of them and says so.

Open the filters and you can narrow the results without typing. Pick one or more
mods to see only their recipes, pick a machine tier, pick the progression tier
that unlocks a recipe, and switch the alternate control to see only alternate
recipes or none of them. Nothing is selected by default, so the palette still
starts with everything the data set contains. The ten special nodes ignore these
controls, because a sink or a storage belongs to no mod and no tier.

Recipes without a known production rate, such as one-off Space Elevator phase
recipes, are kept for existing model references but are absent from the palette
and automatic planning. The modeler does not assign them a made-up cycle time.

The palette is a small panel you can drag out of the way, and it leaves the
canvas visible and usable underneath. It opens with the search field ready, so
you can type straight away. Results are a grid of tiles; the arrow keys move
through them in both directions and Enter places the highlighted one.

The filters sit behind their own toggle, which carries a count while any of
them is on, and every time the palette opens it starts from nothing so a filter
from last time cannot hide what you are looking for.

Pick an entry and it lands where you clicked. Drag from a port and drop on empty
space to open the palette already filtered to what fits that port, then the new
node arrives connected.

Press the question mark key or F1 at any time for the full list of shortcuts.

## Reading a node

Decimal display rounds exact values directly, including very large integers.
Changing the display precision never changes the calculation. If an exact result
is too long for the supported number format, the affected connected group shows
a calculation error. Independent groups still calculate normally.

A node stays compact: its name, its result, and one row per port. Inputs sit on
the left, outputs on the right, and every row names the part it carries and how
much of it moves. Nothing floats over anything else, so a node stays readable
however many ports it has.

The chevron in the header opens the node. That is where the clock, the
Somersloops, an optional limit and the machine choice live, and everything there
is also available in the inspector while the node stays closed.

Solids are counted in items per minute, fluids and gases in cubic metres per
minute, and every value says so. That is the unit the game shows you, so a pipe
carrying 300 m³ reads as 300 and not as 300000.

A recipe node shows the rate it produces. Open it and switch off the per minute
rates to see how many machines that takes instead.

Values are exact fractions internally, so a chain of thirds stays a third
instead of drifting into decimals. The formatted number is what you see, the
exact value sits in the tooltip.

An input with nothing connected to it is red and says so, because nothing
supplies it and no amount of solving will change that.

A node that cannot solve is marked, and the marker says why. You do not have to
hunt through a global list to find out which node broke.

## Clock and Somersloops

Two things change what a node produces, and they do not work the same way.

The clock scales the whole recipe. It reads as a multiplier, so 1 is the rated
speed and 2 is twice it. A machine at 2 eats twice as much and makes twice as
much. Power does not simply follow along: it is raised to the exponent the
building carries, which is why overclocking costs far more than it yields.

The range belongs to the building, not to the modeler. Most factory buildings
run from 0.01 up to 2.5, which is the ceiling three Power Shards give them. Some
stop earlier, and a building the game gives a fixed speed says so instead of
offering a control that cannot do anything. The step buttons move in single
percentage points, and the presets beside them cover the clocks people set by
hand. Only the presets a building can actually reach are offered, so a Modular
Miner is shown nothing above its rated speed.

The summary counts the Power Shards a clock above 1 needs, so a bank running at
250 percent tells you what it costs in shards as well as in power.

Extractors clock the same way. A miner run faster yields more ore and draws the
power for it, inside whatever range its own building allows.

Somersloops are the other half, and they multiply the output alone. What the
machine consumes does not move. That is the whole point of them, and it is why
they are not just another clock.

How much one Somersloop is worth depends on how many slots the building has. A
Constructor has a single slot, so one Somersloop doubles its output. An
Assembler has two, so one reaches 150 percent and both are needed to double it.
A Manufacturer has four, each worth a quarter. Filling every slot always lands
on double, whatever the building. Power climbs steeply here too, so doubling the
output of a machine costs a great deal more than doubling the machine.

Not every building takes Somersloops. Those that do not say so rather than
accepting a number that would change nothing.

Both settings sit on the opened node and in the inspector, so you can reach them
wherever you happen to be working.

Selecting several machines tunes them together. The inspector then offers one
clock and one Somersloop control for the whole selection, each clamped to the
tightest building in it, and the change lands as a single step in the history.
The power the selection draws is shown beside them, which is the number a bank
of machines is usually being tuned against.

## What the solver does

Four modes decide how much the solver is allowed to conclude. None leaves every
value blank while you arrange things. Manual solves the exact equalities you
entered. Basic adds limits and maximizes what flows. Full also honours the order
of your connections when a split has to prioritize.

The solver runs on its own after a change, and a long run can be stopped. Values
it cannot determine stay unknown rather than being guessed.

What a loop returns is used before more of the same material is brought in from
outside. A boiler that feeds a turbine which hands its water back is filled from
that returning line first, and an extractor on the same port only covers what is
left. Wiring the loop is enough; it does not depend on the order of the
connections.

## Special buildings

Beside the recipes the palette carries the nodes that are not recipes: a
splitter and a merger for routing, an outpost and a blueprint for grouping, and
the sink, storage, depot and space elevator. Splitter and merger are the same
free node in two shapes, and you can add further ports to either. Which
connection is served first is the order of the connections on the port, so there
is no separate priority node to choose.

Satisfactory Plus buildings that do not behave like an ordinary recipe get their
own treatment. Refined Power boilers require both their boiler and their heater,
a Slug Hatchery only produces inside its comfortable temperature and humidity, a
farming plot runs faster with a greenhouse module, and an Air Collector produces
from what it can reach.

Where the game data does not describe something completely, the modeler says so
instead of inventing a number. A turbine moves its steam correctly, but the
electricity a generator makes from it depends on values the game data never
ships, so the modeler reports that rather than showing zero.
